## How tissue-culture plantlets arrive

Tissue-culture (TC) aroid plantlets arrive sealed in a bag, still in agar. They are usually about two inches tall, a little smaller or larger depending on the plant, with only a few leaves.

## Unopened bags and flasks must stay below 75°F

This is the one temperature rule that matters and most people do not know it: while plantlets are still sealed in the bag or flask, keep them below 75°F. If tissue-culture plantlets are being stored unplanted for a couple of days or longer, they must stay in a cooler spot, because in the bag they melt if it gets too hot. Once they are out of the bag and planted, ordinary room temperatures are fine.

## Temperature once planted

Nothing specific is done to monitor or control temperature after planting. In a San Diego grow space the plants never sit below about 70°F, usually top out around 80 to 85°F, reach about 88°F on a very hot day, and stay below 90°F.

## Day 0 to day 2: leave them sealed

When plantlets arrive, do not open the bag. Let them sit sealed for a day or two in the spot where they will be grown, so they adjust to that room's temperature and light before anything else changes.

## Deflasking: washing off the agar

After the one-to-two day rest, take the plantlets out of the bag and rinse them under flowing water at the sink. Wash off all of the agar. Peel off any dead leaves. Then soak the plantlets for about fifteen minutes in a bowl of water with a few drops of iodine, very diluted. After the soak, pot them straight into their first container.

## Starting containers: small clear pots under a dome

Plantlets go into very small clear pots, about one and a half inches, set in a 72-cell plant tray with a humidity dome over it. Clear pots matter because root growth through the pot wall is the main sign that acclimation is working.

## Humidity: hold 100 percent for the first week

Starting humidity is always 100 percent, either a single pot under its own dome or a tray with a dome. Hold 100 percent for at least a week. After about a week, start opening the vents or lifting the lid slightly and keep stepping the humidity down slowly from there. Both directions of error hurt: stepping down too slowly delays growth because the plant needs airflow and lower humidity to keep acclimating, and stepping down too fast, or never giving 100 percent at the start, kills plantlets.

## Watering cadence under the dome

Water once at potting. Because humidity sits at 100 percent under the dome, it is usually at least ten days before the plantlets need water again.

## Light: around 150 PPFD for 10 to 12 hours

Light intensity and fixture distance depend on the light, so the target is the reading, not the distance: around 150 PPFD (not an exact figure) for about ten to twelve hours a day, versus at least 250 for established plants. As the plantlets grow taller they get closer to the fixture, which raises their light exposure on its own and matches their growing need for light, so there is no need to turn the light up.

## Signs acclimation is working

Root growth is the main sign. With clear pots, visible roots mean things are going well. A second check when roots are not visible yet: tap the plant lightly, and if it feels more stable in the pot than before, it is rooting. Alocasia can drop every leaf during acclimation and people give up on them, but as long as the center of the plant, the rhizome, is still firm, keep it and keep watering; it will grow back.

## Signs of early failure

Failure shows fast. A plantlet that flops over early is rotting from the center. A center that is soft rather than firm means the plant is lost.

## Leaves melting away after planting is common

After planting from tissue culture, leaves sometimes melt away: they turn to mush and die off. This is pretty common. As long as the base of the plant is fine (the base, not a leaf's stalk), keep taking care of it. If leaves are turning to mush, consider a slight change to watering, because you might be overwatering, and add some daily airflow if you can. Early in the stage, even opening the dome slightly for a few minutes gets fresh air in.

## Yellowing leaves once the dome comes off

Leaves yellowing right after a plant comes out from under the humidity dome are most likely a humidity problem rather than a light problem, usually from taking the dome off too fast. Also make sure the soil is not drying out too much or staying too damp.

## When acclimation is done

There is no single tell. Acclimation is done when the dome or lid can stay completely off and the plant is ready to move up a pot size. In a grow space held at 65 to 80 percent ambient humidity, stepping a Monstera down from 100 percent to 65 percent takes about three weeks, roughly 18 to 21 days, sometimes a little longer. Alocasia take four to five weeks. Anthurium and Philodendron sit in between. A drier room takes longer for all of them.

## Mistakes beginners make most

First: overdoing the start. Distilled water, extra coatings, hormones, and fertilizer on top of each other are not needed, and success rates were higher cleaning the plantlets off under plain running water at the sink. Second: mistiming the humidity step-down, either dragging it out (delayed growth, the plant needs airflow) or yanking the dome off or never starting at 100 percent (kills the plant). Third: potting into a soil that is too dense.

## Up-potting: wait for root-bound, do not disturb the roots

Do not rush the up-pot. Wait until the plant is properly root-bound, with lots of roots circling the pot. Alocasia especially like to be tightly bound. Customers often up-pot the moment a plant looks snug, but most of these plants prefer it and do not want their roots disturbed. When you do up-pot, leave the root ball alone even if the roots are circling everywhere; at most loosen the bottom slightly. Breaking the root ball apart damages the fine root hairs, including ones you cannot see, and the plant takes much longer to recover.
