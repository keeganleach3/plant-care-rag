## pH down on every watering in hard-water areas

San Diego tap water is very hard, so a product literally called pH Down goes into every watering, following the instructions on the bottle, to bring the water to about pH 7 or the high sixes. pH is checked with a cheap meter from Amazon; it measures pH only, not dissolved solids. Anyone elsewhere should look up how hard their local water is and measure their own water before deciding whether they need pH adjustment at all.

## Tap water with conditioner, or rain and reverse osmosis

Tap water with a tap water conditioner added is used for everything here. Many other growers use rainwater or reverse-osmosis water instead to avoid hard-water problems entirely; that is a fine alternative, not a requirement.

## Nutrient lockout: what it looks like and how to avoid it

Nutrient lockout mostly shows up as slower growth. Sometimes there is visible salt buildup on the leaves. To avoid it, one out of every four or five waterings is a heavy top watering with plain water only, no fertilizer, to flush accumulated salts out of the pot. There is no set amount of water per pot; pour until the pot has been thoroughly flushed and drains.

## Fertilizer: any 3-1-2 ratio, plus cal-mag

A varied group of fertilizer products is used here and the brand does not matter. What matters is a 3-1-2 NPK ratio in any multiple: 6-2-4 and 9-3-6 are the same thing. If the fertilizer does not already include calcium and magnesium, add a cal-mag supplement. Dosing depends on the specific brand, so follow that product's label rather than a fixed rule; the standing practice is a light dose with every watering except the flush.

## The two biggest watering and feeding mistakes

First, inconsistent watering: plants swinging back and forth between wet and dry. Second, under-fertilizing. Those two account for most of the slow-growth problems customers bring in.
